<p>Name: The Data Governance Act (2022)</p>
<p>Issuing organisation: EU</p>
<p>Geographical Scope: EU</p>
<p>Type: Initiative</p>
<p>Water management focus: environmental data: combatting climate change, reducing CO₂ emissions and fighting emergencies, such as floods and wildfires;</p>
<p>Description: The EU Data Governance Act (DGA) (Regulation (EU) 2022/868), effective 2023, is an EU framework to boost data sharing and use by creating trust, increasing data availability, and supporting data-driven innovation, focusing on reusing protected public data, regulating neutral data intermediaries, and promoting voluntary data altruism (sharing data for the public good). It sets rules for reusing sensitive public sector data, establishes neutrality requirements for data sharing services (like data marketplaces), and introduces a framework for recognized data altruism organizations, working alongside the GDPR to build a single European data market.</p>
<p>Status: Effective</p>
<p>Effect Date: 2022-06-23 00:00:00</p>

<p>Description: The World Health Organization (WHO) sets global &quot;Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality&quot; (GDWQ) to help countries create national standards, focusing on health risks from chemicals (like arsenic, lead, fluoride) and microbes, alongside aesthetic factors (taste, color). <p>TITLE: Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA)</p>
<p>Name: Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA)</p>
<p>Issuing organisation: USA</p>
<p>Geographical Scope: USA</p>
<p>Type: Legistative</p>
<p>Water management focus: Drinking Water</p>
<p>Description: The Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) was originally passed by Congress in 1974 to protect public health by regulating the nation’s public drinking water supply. The law was amended in 1986 and 1996 and requires many actions to protect drinking water and its sources—rivers, lakes, reservoirs, springs, and ground water wells. (SDWA does not regulate private wells which serve fewer than 25 individuals.) SDWA authorizes the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) to set national health-based standards for drinking water to protect against both naturally-occurring and man-made contaminants that may be found in drinking water. US EPA, states, and water systems then work together to make sure that these standards are met.</p>
<p>Status: Effective</p>
<p>Effect Date: 1974</p>
<p>Resource: https://www.epa.gov/sdwa</p>

<p>TITLE: Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals</p>
<p>Name: Secondary Drinking Water Standards: Guidance for Nuisance Chemicals</p>
<p>Issuing organisation: USA</p>
<p>Geographical Scope: USA</p>
<p>Type: Guidelines/ Standards</p>
<p>Description: In addition, EPA has established National Secondary Drinking Water Standards that set non-mandatory water quality standards for 15 contaminants. EPA does not enforce these &quot;secondary maximum contaminant levels&quot; (SMCLs). They are established as guidelines to assist public water systems in managing their drinking water for aesthetic considerations, such as taste, color, and odor. These contaminants are not considered to present a risk to human health at the SMCL. While SMCLs are not federally enforceable, EPA requires a special notice for exceedance of the fluoride SMCL of 2.0 mg/L. Community water systems that exceed the fluoride SMCL of 2 mg/L, but do not exceed the MCL of 4.0 mg/L for fluoride, must provide public notice to persons served no later than 12 months from the day the water system learns of the exceedance (40 CFR 141.208).</p>
